## Our approach to communication

Our writing should reflect who we are: technically capable, philosophically different, independent, thoughtful, and focused on solving real problems. We want people to understand what we do, why it matters, and what makes our approach different.

We don't need complicated language, excessive jargon, or inflated claims to demonstrate technical knowledge. Our expertise should be evident in the substance of what we say, not the complexity of how we say it.

Above all, our communication should feel human, credible, and approachable.

## Our voice: how we communicate

Our communication is shaped by our brand promise: **technology should give people control, not take it away.** Reliable infrastructure and meaningful ownership give people the freedom to build, adapt, and grow on their terms.

Our voice reflects that belief through four defining attributes:

<p class="patmos-headline-s">Authentic, inclusive, serious, & quietly bold.</p>

| Attribute | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Authentic | Be genuine and accountable. We say what we mean and communicate like knowledgeable people talking to other people. We avoid corporate language, borrowed buzzwords, and inflated claims. We're transparent about what we do, what we can deliver, and where we can improve. |
| Inclusive | Be clear and approachable. We make complex subjects understandable without oversimplifying them. We use technical language when it adds value, explain it when necessary, and never talk down to our audience. |
| Serious | Be precise and useful. The infrastructure we provide supports critical workloads, and our communication should reflect that responsibility. We favor facts, meaningful details, and practical explanations over vague promises. |
| Quietly bold | Be confident without the hype. We have strong convictions and aren't afraid to challenge conventional thinking. We communicate those beliefs directly, support our claims with evidence, and let our work speak for itself. |

### Write conversationally

Use natural language, straightforward sentences, and an approachable tone. Contractions are welcome. Avoid unnecessarily formal language and elaborate explanations that don't add value.

<Compare
  avoid="Patmos leverages its comprehensive portfolio of innovative infrastructure solutions to facilitate optimal business outcomes."
  prefer="We help customers build and operate the infrastructure they need, with the flexibility to adapt as their requirements change."
/>

### Speak in first person

When communicating on behalf of Patmos, use we, our, and us instead of repeatedly referring to Patmos in the third person.

<Compare
  avoid="Patmos works closely with customers to design infrastructure solutions."
  prefer="We work closely with customers to design infrastructure around their requirements."
/>

Third-person references are appropriate when introducing Patmos, writing press releases or independent editorial content, and referring to named products, facilities, or business units.

### Address the reader

Use you and your when appropriate, particularly in customer-facing content. Focus on the reader's challenges, priorities, and opportunities rather than describing ourselves endlessly. Speak to them, not about us.

<Compare
  avoid="Our dedicated infrastructure services offer multiple configuration options."
  prefer="Choose the hardware and configuration that fit your workloads."
/>

### Be technical, but accessible

Technical expertise is one of our strengths, but our readers shouldn't need to be experts to understand what we're saying. Use accurate terminology and meaningful technical details while making the message accessible for our intended audience.

Spell out acronyms on first use. Avoid unnecessary buzzwords, and explain jargon when it may be unfamiliar or have multiple interpretations. A reader should never have to leave our content to look up terminology to understand the message.

### Be confident without exaggerating

Communicate our strengths directly, but avoid hyperbole, empty superlatives, and claims we can't substantiate. When specific, verified results are available, use them.

<Compare
  avoid="Our revolutionary infrastructure delivers unmatched deployment speeds."
  prefer="Our approach to design and deployment can reduce the time required to prepare infrastructure for customer workloads."
/>

## Our messaging: what we communicate

Our messaging should consistently reinforce what makes Patmos different and why it matters to the people we serve. Not every message needs to include every brand theme. Focus on what's relevant to the audience, supported by facts, and useful to their decision.

### Core messaging themes

| Theme | How to use it |
| --- | --- |
| Built around customer requirements | We design and configure infrastructure around what customers need to accomplish. Start with the workload, technical requirements, and business objectives. |
| Independent by design | Emphasize the flexibility, choice, and independence our approach enables, supported by tangible benefits rather than broad claims about freedom. |
| Technical expertise and accountability | Demonstrate expertise through technical details, practical examples, and verified customer outcomes. |
| Partnership over transactions | Show what collaboration looks like in practice, from early planning and design through deployment and ongoing operations. |
| Responsible growth and community impact | Our Technology Campus model includes adaptive reuse, economic opportunity, and responsible resource management. Support these claims with real examples and measurable outcomes. |
| Freedom as a service | Connect ownership, transparency, meaningful choice, and control to practical customer benefits and the commitments in our Hosting Bill of Rights. |

### Build messages around value, not just capabilities

Whenever possible, connect four elements:

1. **Customer need:** what problem are we solving or opportunity are we addressing?
2. **Our approach:** what do we actually do?
3. **Differentiation:** what makes our approach meaningful?
4. **Outcome:** what does the customer gain?

Not every paragraph needs all four elements, but the overall content should connect capabilities to customer value.

## Editorial and style standards

These conventions apply across Patmos content unless a specific publication format requires otherwise.

| Topic | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Headings | Sentence case, not title case. |
| Brand perspective | Use we, our, and us when speaking on behalf of Patmos. |
| Reader perspective | Use you and your when appropriate to speak directly to the audience. |
| Dashes | Use a spaced en dash ( – ) rather than an em dash. |
| Acronyms and jargon | Spell out acronyms on first use. Explain specialized terminology when its meaning may not be clear. |
| Data center | Always two words, never datacenter. |
| Measurement | Include a space between numbers and units: 10 MW, 750 kW. |
| Hyphenation | Hyphenate compound modifiers: high-density infrastructure, purpose-built facility. |
| Numbers | Use numerals for measurements, percentages, financial figures, and technical specifications. |
| Capitalization | Capitalize proper nouns, official product names, and established brand terminology only. |
| Voice | Prefer active voice over passive constructions. |
| Punctuation | Use the serial comma. Avoid excessive exclamation points. Use ’s to form a possessive when a singular noun ends in s: Patmos's, not Patmos'. |
| Paragraphs | Keep paragraphs short and easy to scan. |
| Calls to action | Clear, conversational, and action-oriented: "Let's talk support" rather than "Talk to sales." |

## Terminology and word choice

Avoid corporate buzzwords, vague marketing language, and terminology that makes straightforward ideas unnecessarily complicated.

Use technical terminology accurately and consistently. Distinctions between colocation, dedicated infrastructure, cloud, Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), and GPU as a Service (GPUaaS) matter. Don't treat related products or services as interchangeable simply to make the writing more accessible.

When specialized terminology is necessary, provide enough context for the intended audience to understand its meaning without having to research it elsewhere.

## Writing effective content

Good content helps readers understand a problem, evaluate an opportunity, or make a decision.

- **Lead with something meaningful.** Start with a customer need, relevant opportunity, important fact, or clear point of view.
- **Show rather than claim.** Support messages with technical details, customer examples, measurable outcomes, and evidence. Link to supporting evidence where you can.
- **Make differentiation tangible.** Explain what we do differently, why it matters, and how it benefits the customer.
- **Respect the audience.** Adapt detail and context for engineers, executives, investors, developers, and community stakeholders without changing our voice.
- **Give the reader a logical next step.** "Watch the interview" is more useful than a generic "Learn more."

  prefer="By aligning design, power distribution, cooling, and customer requirements early, we've supported rack-ready deployments in approximately 90 days."

## Applying our voice across content

| Format | Approach |
| --- | --- |
| Website and product pages | Lead with customer needs and outcomes. Help visitors quickly determine whether our solutions fit their requirements. |
| Campaigns and emails | Be direct, relevant, and conversational. Focus on one primary message with a clear call to action. |
| Case studies | Tell the customer's story through objectives, challenges, our approach, and measurable outcomes. |
| Thought leadership | Share informed perspectives, supported by experience, evidence, and practical insight. |
| Press and corporate | Be factual and clear. Lead with the news and distinguish confirmed developments from future plans. |
| Community | Be transparent, respectful, and accessible. Address concerns directly. |
| Technical content | Prioritize accuracy, useful detail, and logical organization. |

## Accuracy and credibility

Verify technical specifications, capacity and availability, deployment timelines, certifications, customer outcomes, financial figures, and environmental statements before publishing. Be especially careful to distinguish between:

- **Operational and planned capacity:** what's operational today versus what's under development, planned, or proposed.
- **Confirmed and projected outcomes:** what we've demonstrated or committed to versus what we expect to achieve.
- **General capabilities and customer-specific commitments:** what our infrastructure can support versus what has been designed, contracted, or guaranteed for a particular engagement.
- **Verified environmental benefits and sustainability goals:** what we've measured or established versus what we're working toward.
- **Actual customer results and anticipated benefits:** what customers have achieved versus what our approach is intended to help them accomplish.

When discussing future plans, estimates, or potential outcomes, use language that makes their status clear. Don't present possibilities as commitments or individual project results as universal capabilities.

We should never sacrifice technical accuracy to make a message sound more compelling.

## Final content checklist

Before publishing or sharing content, ask:

- **Voice:** does it sound authentic, inclusive, serious, and quietly bold?
- **Clarity:** is it easy to understand without unnecessary jargon or unexplained acronyms?
- **Value:** does it explain why the information matters to the reader?
- **Differentiation:** does it show what makes our approach meaningful?
- **Accuracy:** are facts and claims verified and supported by evidence?
- **Consistency:** does it follow our editorial standards and terminology?
- **Relationship-driven:** does it reflect transparency, accountability, and customer relationships?
- **Action:** is the purpose clear, with a relevant next step?

If the content meets these standards, it should feel recognizably Patmos.
